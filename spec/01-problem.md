# 01 — The Problem

## The platform tax

Every major marketplace runs the same economics:

1. The platform charges the seller a commission. Food delivery takes 15 to 30 percent. Ride hailing takes 30 to 45 percent effectively. Amazon sellers lose 30 to 35 percent all-in once referral and fulfillment fees combine.
2. The seller cannot absorb it, so they raise prices on the platform. Studies find 70 to 80 percent of restaurants raise menu prices 15 to 25 percent on delivery apps. Total delivery markups reach 69 to 92 percent once fees, service charges, and tips stack up.
3. The buyer pays the difference for the identical item. The seller takes the blame for prices they did not choose.

## The lock-in

The commission is only half the problem. The platform also owns:

- **Demand.** Buyers open the platform's app, not the seller's.
- **Identity.** The seller's profile exists inside the platform.
- **Reputation.** Ratings and reviews cannot be taken elsewhere.

A seller who leaves starts from zero. A buyer who leaves loses their history. Neither side can credibly threaten to walk away, so the platform can raise the tax at will. This is why the pattern never self-corrects: the market power sits with whoever controls discovery and identity, not with whoever has the best prices.

## Why fixes so far have failed

- **Price parity clauses** (platforms forbidding sellers from charging less elsewhere) treat the symptom and entrench the disease. They have triggered antitrust lawsuits.
- **Commission caps** (New York City's 15 percent delivery cap, now permanent) help sellers but platforms re-layer the cost as buyer-side junk fees instead of absorbing it. The buyer still pays.
- **A better app** with lower fees faces the cold-start problem with no structural advantage: incumbents respond with subsidies, exclusive contracts, and acquisitions.

None of these change who owns discovery, identity, and reputation. FairMarket is designed to change exactly that.
